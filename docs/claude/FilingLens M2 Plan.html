Where we stand

28 questions out of 51 go to the LLM without the passage they need

When the passage isn't in those 6 chunks, the LLM can only answer "not found" or guess. So M2 is about one thing: ranking. Getting the right chunk from rank 15 or 40 up into the top 6.

Three ideas, tested before building

One idea is worth a lot. The other two, as a first try, are not.

I ran a quick prototype on all 51 questions. It changed nothing in the database: keyword search was computed on the fly.

Idea 1 · search by words

A smarter Ctrl+F, built into Postgres

In simple termsSearch by meaning (what we have) compares fingerprints: "cost of revenue" and "COGS" can match even with no shared words. Search by words is the opposite: it only finds chunks that contain the question's actual words, but it finds them reliably. Postgres has this built in. It ignores small words ("the", "of") and word endings, so "statements" matches "statement".
How it works in Postgres
  • Each chunk gets a list of its words, stemmed: a tsvector column, filled automatically.
  • An index on that column (a GIN index), like an index on any column you search by.
  • The question becomes a tsquery, and Postgres scores every chunk by how many of the words it contains and how close together they are (ts_rank_cd).
Why it failed on average

These are the words sent for the Microsoft COGS question. Teal words help. Struck-out words are instructions to the answerer, but the search treats them as content, and "millions" or "provide" appear in hundreds of chunks. Grey words are dropped by Postgres anyway.

    Idea 2 · merge the two lists

    Reciprocal rank fusion: points for being high on either list

    In simple termsEach search gives a ranked list. Every chunk earns points from each list: 1 ÷ (60 + its rank). Add the points, sort again. A chunk that is decent on both lists beats one that is first on one list and nowhere on the other. It only uses ranks, so it doesn't matter that the two searches score on different scales.
    Read it like this

    The 60 is the standard constant from the original paper. It keeps one first place from outweighing everything else.

    Merging can only help when the word search is good. As a first try it isn't, so step 2 of the plan cleans the question before searching by words.

    Idea 3 · search the right section

    Most answers live in two Items

    Why it works so well

    Item 8 is about a third of a filing's chunks (89 of 260 on average). Searching only there removes two thirds of the competition. Boeing's legal question: rank 11 → 1. AMD's quick ratio: rank 18 → 2.

    The hard part

    36/51 assumed we always know the right Item. A real system has to guess it from the question, and a wrong guess hides the answer completely. So the search should fall back to all sections when unsure.

    How to guess the sectionHow it worksForAgainst
    RulesWords in the question pick the Items: "risk" → 1A; "legal", "lawsuit" → 1A, 3, 8; numbers, ratios, "balance sheet" → 7 + 8Free, instant, easy to testBrittle: questions phrase things in many ways
    Small LLM callA cheap model reads the question and returns a list of likely ItemsUnderstands phrasing; this is what the M3 agent's "plan" step will do anywayOne extra call per question (~0.5 s, a fraction of a cent)
    Soft boostSearch everything, but rank chunks from the likely Items higherA wrong guess costs a few ranks, not the answerSmaller gain than a hard filter

    The plan

    Six steps, each measured

    1

    Word index in Postgres

    A tsvector column on chunk, filled automatically by Postgres, plus a GIN index. One migration. No new library.

    Done when keyword search over a filing takes milliseconds and gives the same results as the prototype.

    2

    Clean the question before searching by words

    Drop instruction words ("please", "answer", "provide", "round", "units") so only content words are searched.

    Measure word search alone: does it beat 17/51?

    3

    Merge both searches (RRF)

    hybrid_search(): run both, merge the ranks, return the top k.

    Measure merged vs meaning alone (23/51). Keep it only if it wins.

    4

    Section guessing

    Try rules and a small LLM call, as a hard filter and as a soft boost, with a fallback to all sections.

    Measure each against today (23/51) and the ceiling (36/51).

    5

    Citations

    Results and answers carry company, form, year, Item and filing URL. RAG prompt v2 asks for [n] citations tied to those sources.

    Done when every RAG answer's sources list the Item and the link.

    6

    Final measurement

    Retrieval on the 51 questions, then one full run with the LLM and the judge on the 20, compared with plain RAG (6/20) and closed-book (11/20).

    Fair test try ideas on 31 of the 51 questions, confirm the winner once on the other 20.

    Why split the questionsIf we try ten variants on the same 51 questions and keep the best, part of the "improvement" is luck: we picked whatever happened to fit these 51. Choosing on 31 questions and confirming on 20 the choice never saw gives an honest number. It's the same idea as a train/test split.

    Everything here is code in this repo and Postgres features. The only new cost is the section-guessing LLM call, if we keep it.

    Check yourself

    Interview questions for M2

    What is hybrid search, and why combine two searches?

    Running a search by meaning (embeddings) and a search by words (full-text) and merging the results. Meaning handles different wording ("COGS" vs "cost of revenue"); words handle exact terms, names and numbers that embeddings blur. Each catches misses of the other, when both are decent.

    How do you merge two ranked lists with different score scales?

    Reciprocal rank fusion: every item gets 1 / (60 + rank) from each list, and the points are added. It only uses positions, so cosine distances and text-match scores never have to be compared.

    Your hybrid search scored the same as vector search. Why could that be?

    Merging only helps if both lists are good. Here the word search was weak (17/51) because the questions contain instruction words that match almost every chunk. Fix the weaker list first, then merge.

    What does a "ceiling" experiment tell you?

    How much an idea could give if one hard part were solved perfectly. Searching only the right section gave 36/51 vs 23/51, which says section routing is worth building and how much is at stake. The real result will be lower, because the router will sometimes guess wrong.

    Why tune on some questions and test on others?

    Trying many variants on one set and keeping the best overfits to that set: part of the gain is luck. Choosing on one part and confirming once on a held-out part gives a number you can trust.
