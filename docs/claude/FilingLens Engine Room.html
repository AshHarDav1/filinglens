What happens when you ask FilingLens a question

Two pipelines share one Postgres database. Ingestion runs once per filing and writes. The question path runs every time and only reads. Every number and row on this page comes from your local database and your eval runs.

The whole system

Two pipelines, one database

Storage

Four tables, and what every column is for

The chain is company → filing → chunk: one company has many filings, one filing has many chunks. embedding_cache stands to the side. It has no foreign key; it is matched by the hash of a chunk's text. Examples are real rows, mostly the American Express FY2022 10-K.

The question path

One question, end to end

A real question from the eval, replayed from your rag-poc run: “How much has the effective tax rate of American Express changed between FY2021 and FY2022?”

The question comes with a company and a year

STEP 3 · FENCE AND RANK

One SQL query does the whole search

The WHERE clause is the fence: it narrows 2,922 chunks to the 373 from this filing, using normal B-tree indexes. Then <=>, pgvector's cosine distance operator, scores each of the 373 against the question, and ORDER BY … LIMIT 6 keeps the closest. There is no vector index, so this is an exact scan. At this size it takes milliseconds.

retrieval/vector.py · search() 
SELECT chunk.*, filing.*, company.*,
       chunk.embedding <=> :question_vector AS distance
FROM chunk
JOIN filing  ON chunk.filing_id   = filing.id
JOIN company ON filing.company_id = company.id
WHERE company.ticker = 'AXP'          -- the fence:
  AND filing.fiscal_year = 2022       -- 2,922 → 373 rows
ORDER BY distance                     -- closest meaning first
LIMIT 6;

Inside step 3

How the search decides, and how it misses

Think of each vector as an arrow in a 1,536-dimensional space. Cosine distance measures the angle between the question's arrow and a chunk's arrow: 0 means they point the same way, around 1 means unrelated. Postgres computes that for every chunk inside the fence, sorts, and keeps the first six.

Every chunk of a 10-K is about money, so the distances crowd together between roughly 0.35 and 0.83. Small differences at the top decide what the LLM gets to read. Below, each tick is one chunk of the filing, placed at its distance from the question.

  • Different words. The question says “COGS”. The filing says “Total cost of revenue”.
  • Numbers carry no meaning to an embedding model. HTML tables were flattened into one cell per line, so most of this chunk is bare figures.
  • The chunk mixes two sections. A fixed 2,000-character window started in Item 7A (market risk) and ran into Item 8, so its vector is an average of two topics.
  • The question carries instructions. “Please state answer in USD millions” is embedded too, and pulls the search toward chunks that read like instructions.

Your rag-poc run had 10 of 20 answers come back as “Not found in the filings.” Five of them were checked by hand, and in all five the answer was in the database but outside the top six. When the right chunk was retrieved, answers were exact. Retrieval is the bottleneck.

Design decisions

Why it is built this way

Decision

Vectors live in Postgres

One query can filter by company and year, join to the filing, and rank by meaning. One database to run, back up and migrate.

Trade-off: a dedicated vector database scales further. That matters at many millions of vectors, not at 2,922.

ADR-001
Decision

Fence before ranking

Similarity search always returns something. Without the WHERE ticker, year fence, an AMD question could be answered from Microsoft's text.

Trade-off: questions across companies or years need an explicit plan. The agent handles that in M3.

Decision

Fixed 2,000-character chunks

The simplest chunker that works, so the PoC could measure something quickly.

Trade-off: tables split mid-row and sections get mixed, as chunk 115 shows. Section-aware chunking comes in M1.

Decision

Six chunks per question

A small prompt: about 3,000 tokens, fast and cheap, and less noise for the LLM to ignore.

Trade-off: recall. The Microsoft answer sat at rank 73. Trying k = 10, 20, 40 is the next cheap experiment.

Decision

Raw files on disk, one transaction per filing

Parsing can change without downloading from SEC again. Pressing Ctrl+C never leaves half a filing in the database.

Trade-off: 52 MB of HTML on disk, outside git.

Decision

Cache embeddings by text hash and model

Rechunking or re-running only pays for text that is new.

Trade-off: every vector is stored twice (24 MB). Cheap insurance at this size.

Decision

Numbers will come from XBRL

The Microsoft miss is the reason. Tables lose their structure as text, so figures like cost of revenue will come from SEC's structured XBRL data through get_financials. Text search stays for the “why”.

Trade-off: a second ingestion path and a concept mapping (COGS → CostOfRevenue) to maintain.
