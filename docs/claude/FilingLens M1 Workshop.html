Part A · Steps 1–2

Numbers: stop searching for them, look them up

XBRL is a tagging standard: when a company files a 10-K, every number in its financial statements is also submitted as a machine-readable fact with a standard name (a concept, like CostOfRevenue), a period, a unit and a value. SEC publishes all of a company's facts as one JSON file, companyfacts.

So the number we failed to find with RAG has been sitting in a structured API the whole time.

Backend analogyScraping prices out of a shop's HTML versus calling the shop's JSON API. RAG on tables is the scraper. XBRL is the API.
Three traps in this data
  • Duplicates. Every number is reported again in the next one or two 10-Ks as a comparison column. One period can have 3 rows.
  • fy lies. It is the fiscal year of the filing that reported the fact, not of the period the fact describes. Filter on the period dates.
  • Concepts get renamed. Microsoft tagged cost of revenue as CostOfRevenue until its FY2017 10-K, and as CostOfGoodsAndServicesSold from FY2018 on. A metric is a list of concepts, not one name.

Plus one more you'll meet: balance-sheet numbers (debt, current liabilities) are instant facts with only an end date. Income-statement numbers are duration facts with start and end.

  Part B · Step 3
  
Sections: teach the text where it came from

  

    
Every 10-K follows the same legal outline: Item 1 Business, Item 1A Risk Factors, Item 7 MD&A (management's explanation), Item 7A market risk, Item 8 Financial Statements, and so on up to Item 16. The M0 chunk 115 failed partly because it was half Item 7A and half Item 8. If we split the document at Item boundaries first, no chunk can mix two sections, and every chunk gets a section label we can filter on in M2.

  

  

  

    

      Why a regex for "Item 8" is not enough
      
    
        
  • The table of contents comes first. Every heading appears once near the top, a few lines apart, before the real one.
    • 
        
  • Running page headers. Microsoft prints "PART II Item 8" at the top of every page: 46 matches for Item 8 in the FY2016 10-K.
    • 
        
  • Cross-references. Sentences like "see Item 7" mid-paragraph.
    • 
        
  • Odd layouts. AmEx has no table of contents that looks like headings. Netflix and PepsiCo keep statements outside Item 8.
    • 
      

    

    

      A strategy that works on all 12
      
    
        
  • Rule 1. Match heading lines (^Item 7A. at a line start), not words in sentences.
    • 
        
  • Rule 2. Skip the table of contents: the body starts at the first "Item 1" whose next heading is at least ~400 characters away.
    • 
        
  • Rule 3. Walk forward and accept a heading only if it comes later in the outline than the current one. A repeated "Item 8" while you're in Item 8 is a page header; ignore it.
    • 
        
  • Rule 4. Label, don't drop. Keep every section; filtering is a query-time choice.
    •

  Part C · Step 4
  
Tables: keep each row on one line

  
In the 10-K's HTML, every table cell contains its own <p> or <div>, and our converter turns each of those into a line break. The fix: inside a table, join cells with | and end the line only at the end of a row. That's parsing plumbing, so I write it.

  

    
    
  

  

    

    
  

  
Rows alone barely change the embedding (see Part D). They matter because the LLM can now read "Total cost of revenue | 32,780" as one fact, and because M2's keyword search will match "cost of revenue" next to its number.







  Part D · Step 5
  
Chunks that say what they are

  

    
A contextual header is one line glued to the top of every chunk before it is embedded: company, form, fiscal year, section. A table of numbers says almost nothing to an embedding model. The same table under "Microsoft · 10-K FY2016 · Item 8 · Income Statements" says exactly what it is. This technique is often called contextual retrieval.

The experiment: what each fix does to the Microsoft COGS chunk

  
I re-embedded chunk 115 in four versions, each adding one M1 fix, and measured its rank among the other 202 chunks of the same filing. Switch between the original eval question and a cleaned-up query (the kind of rewrite the M3 agent will do).

  

    
    
  

  
  

    Update after step 6: the full measurement overturned this. With every chunk carrying a header, hit@6 dropped from 8/20 to 6/20. The header still pulled far misses closer (Microsoft COGS rank 73 → 23), but it pushed good hits down (Adobe 9 → 48, Verizon 14 → 45). The reason: search is already filtered to one filing, so "Microsoft · FY2016" is identical on every candidate chunk. It tells them apart from nothing and only makes them look alike. In this one-chunk test, only chunk 115 had the header, which made it look like a win.
  

  
The lesson worth repeating in an interview: an experiment on one item, with everything else held at the old version, can point the wrong way. Measure on the whole set.


  






  Part E · Step 6
  
Measure, or it didn't happen

  
Same 20 questions, one matcher for every variant. I rebuilt the chunks three ways from the same filings and ranked the FinanceBench evidence under each. This is called an ablation: change one thing at a time and measure.

No gain at hit@6 (8/20 → 8/20), and the reasons to keep it are structural: no chunk crosses two Items, every chunk carries its Item for M2's section filters, and the LLM reads Total cost of revenue | 32,780 instead of loose numbers.

    

    
Where the real retrieval gains must come from
      
The ranking itself. Four questions are still beyond rank 40. Two of them (Microsoft COGS and debt) are now answered by XBRL lookups. The rest is M2's work: keyword search next to vector search, and section filters.

  Check yourself
  
Interview questions for M1

  
Answer out loud before you open each one.

  

    
Why do numbers come from XBRL and not from RAG?
Numbers in tables embed badly (rank 73 for the Microsoft income statement), and an LLM reading a number off a flattened table can misread the row or the column. XBRL gives the exact value with its period, unit and source filing. Lookup beats search when the data is structured; RAG is for the explanations around the numbers.

    
A fact has fy = 2017 but covers July 2015 to June 2016. Why, and what do you filter on?
fy is the fiscal year of the filing that reported it: the FY2017 10-K repeats the prior year as a comparison column. Filter on the period dates (computing the fiscal year from period_end), and pick one row per period.

    
Why can't you find sections with a simple regex for "Item 8"?
The heading also appears in the table of contents, as a running page header on every page (46 times in Microsoft's FY2016 10-K), and in cross-references. You need structure: skip the TOC, and only accept headings that move forward through the outline.

    
What is a contextual chunk header, and did it help here?
A short line with company, year and section prepended to each chunk before embedding. On one chunk it looked like a big win (rank 73 to 2), but measured on all 20 questions it lowered hit@6 from 8/20 to 6/20. Our search is already filtered to one filing, so company and year are the same on every candidate chunk and only make them look alike. It helps when you search across many documents, where that context actually tells chunks apart. Here we show the context to the LLM in the prompt instead.

    
Why label every section instead of dropping the ones you don't need?
Because filings don't follow the outline perfectly: Netflix's statements sit after Item 15, PepsiCo's inside Item 7. Dropping by section would silently lose data. Labels let the query decide, and a wrong label costs ranking, not data.

    
You changed the chunking. How do you know retrieval got better?
Re-run the retrieval hit-rate metric on the same questions and compare it with the same metric on the old chunks: M0's hit@6, 8/20 with the current matcher. Both sides skip the LLM: embed the question, search the top 6, and check whether the FinanceBench evidence text is inside them. That makes it cheap enough to run after every change. Don't compare it with answer accuracy (RAG 6/20, closed-book 11/20), which is a different measurement: retrieval is necessary for a right answer but not sufficient, so confirm with the full judged eval at milestones.
